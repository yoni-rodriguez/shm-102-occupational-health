# The Black Death

![Nicolas Poussin, *The Plague of Ashdod*, c. 1630.](the-plague-of-ashdod-nicolas-poussin-1630.jpg){fig-alt="Painting of a city during a plague outbreak, showing sick and dying people in the streets, grieving figures, and classical architecture."}

The Black Death swept through **Europe, western Asia, and Africa from 1346 to 1352**.

It became one of the greatest public health disasters in recorded history.

For more than **500 years**, people could observe the disease and its devastating effects without knowing exactly what caused it or how it spread.

# Competing Explanations

People could observe disease long before they could identify its cause.

Without the ability to see microorganisms, physicians and communities developed different explanations for why people became sick.

Ideas included:

- divine punishment
- imbalance within the body
- environmental conditions
- contaminated water
- poisonous or corrupted air
- contagion between people

Some observations were meaningful.

The challenge was explaining **why** those patterns occurred.

:::::: columns
::: {.column width="48%"}
## Imbalance Within the Body

For centuries, European medicine was strongly influenced by the **theory of the four humors**.

Health was thought to depend on maintaining a balance among:

- blood
- yellow bile
- black bile
- phlegm

Illness could therefore be interpreted as an **imbalance within the body**.

Treatments intended to restore balance included bloodletting and purging.
:::

::: {.column width="4%"}
:::

## What If the Problem Was in the Air?

Another influential explanation associated disease with **miasma**, or corrupted air.

Illness was frequently observed alongside:

- overcrowding
- waste
- decomposition
- stagnant water
- poor sanitation
- unpleasant odors

These observations were not imaginary.

**The environmental conditions were real.**

What remained uncertain was the mechanism connecting those conditions to disease.

:::::: columns
::: {.column width="58%"}
During plague outbreaks, people attempted to protect themselves from dangerous air in different ways.

The familiar image of the **plague doctor** reflects this history. Plague doctors stuffed the bird-like beaks with strong-smelling herbs and spices to protect themselves from what they believed spread disease: foul, disease-carrying air known as **miasma**, or "bad air."

People were trying to control an exposure.

They had not yet identified the actual hazard.
:::

:::

## Do Old Ideas Completely Disappear?

Historical explanations of disease did not simply vanish when scientific understanding changed.

People continue to search for explanations when the causes of illness are uncertain, frightening, or difficult to observe.

The explanations differ across cultures, communities, and time.

The scientific challenge remains the same:

### What evidence supports the explanation?

## Seeds of Contagion

Not everyone explained disease through bodily imbalance or corrupted air.

As plague spread through medieval Europe, people increasingly recognized that the disease appeared to be **contagious**.

::: {.callout-note title="The Unanswered Questions"}
**What was being transmitted?**

**How was it being transmitted?**
:::

In the sixteenth century, Italian physician **Girolamo Fracastoro "Giro" (1478–1533)** proposed that plague and other diseases could be transmitted by invisible **"seeds of contagion,"** or *seminaria*.

This was a very different hypothesis that asked whether disease was transmitted by something physical that people simply could not see.

# The Microscope

In the seventeenth century, improvements in microscopy allowed scientists to observe structures and organisms that had previously been invisible.

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="52%"}

**Robert Hooke (1635–1703)** used a compound microscope to examine biological materials and published his observations in *Micrographia* in 1665.

Hooke observed microscopic structures and organisms, including mold, but his microscopes were not powerful enough to reveal individual bacteria.

**Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723)** developed powerful single-lens microscopes and became the first person known to observe single-celled microorganisms, eventually including bacteria.

::: {.callout-important title="Seeing Was Not the Same as Understanding"}
**Humans could finally see microorganisms.**

**However, nearly two centuries passed before scientists established the connection between microorganisms and disease.**
:::

# Louis Pasteur

Remember where this lecture began?

**France.**

French chemist **Louis Pasteur (1822–1895)** investigated microorganisms while studying problems involving fermentation for the French Beer & Wine Industry.

His research demonstrated that yeast, a single-celled fungus, in the absence of oxygen produced alcohol as a terminal waste product.

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="45%"}

In his famous **swan-neck flask experiments**, air could enter a sterilized flask of beef broth, while dust and microorganisms were trapped in the curved neck.

The broth remained free of microbial growth for many years.

When a flask was tilted so that the broth contacted material trapped in the neck, microorganisms grew.

**The air was present.**

**Microbial growth was not.**

:::

::: {.column width="5%"}
:::

::: {.column width="50%"}

![CLICK TO ENLARGE | Louis Pasteur and the swan-neck flask experiment. Source: Slonczewski, Foster, and Zinser, *Microbiology: An Evolving Science*, 6th ed., Figure 1.14.](louis-pasteur.jpg){.lightbox fig-alt="Portrait of Louis Pasteur beside a diagram of his swan-neck flask experiment showing sterilization of broth, microbes trapped in the curved neck, and microbial growth after broth contacts the trapped material." width="100%"}

:::
::::

::: {.callout-tip title="From Observation to Experiment"}
This represented more than simply observing microorganisms.

Scientists could use **controlled experiments to test competing explanations**.

1. *Make an observation*
2. *Ask a question*
3. *Form a hypothesis*
4. *Conduct an experiment*
5. *Evaluate the evidence*
:::

## Germ Theory

Microscopy made microorganisms visible.

Experimental science made it possible to investigate what microorganisms were doing.

During the nineteenth century, evidence from scientists including **Louis Pasteur** and **Robert Koch** helped establish the **germ theory of disease**.

The central idea was revolutionary:

### Many infectious diseases are caused by microorganisms.

Pasteur's experiments demonstrated that microorganisms did not "simply appear" through what was considered spontaneous generation.

Koch later developed methods for studying microorganisms in isolation and establishing causal relationships between specific microorganisms and specific diseases.

::: {.callout-tip title="A New Scientific Framework"}
Scientists now had:

- **a scientific framework**
- **experimental methods**
- **technologies for observing microorganisms**

Together, these developments changed the questions scientists could ask.

**Germ theory:** the theory that many infectious diseases are caused by microorganisms.
:::

## Following the Evidence

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="55%"}

Yersin examined the bubo fluid under a microscope.

He found it filled with bacteria.

Now there was a candidate organism.

But seeing bacteria in a sick person did not automatically prove that the bacteria caused plague.

Yersin used material from the bubo to inoculate guinea pigs.

The guinea pigs died.

When he examined them, he found the same bacteria.

The bacterium responsible for plague would eventually be named:

### *Yersinia pestis*

in recognition of Alexandre Yersin.

# Back to 1894

### What Had Yersin Solved?

Yersin had answered an enormous question.

### What causes plague?

**A bacterium: *Yersinia pestis*.**

::: {.callout-note title="One Question Remained"}
But another question remained unanswered.

**How was the bacterium moving between rats and humans?**

Knowing the **hazard** did not automatically reveal the **exposure pathway**.
:::


We know the agent.

We know humans and rats can be infected.

But we still do not know the exposure pathway.

::: {.callout-note title="The Next Investigation"}
If rats and humans can carry the same organism, how would you determine **how the organism moves from rats to humans?**

Yersin had connected plague with rats.

Another investigator would take the next major step.
:::

# Paul Louis Simond

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="60%"}

French army physician **Paul Louis Simond** traveled to Vietnam and India to follow up on Yersin's observations.

He noticed an important occupational health pattern.

Twenty laborers in a wool factory had been cleaning dead rats from the floor.

**Those 20 workers died of plague.**

Other factory workers who had **no contact with the rats** did not become ill.

That observation suggested an exposure relationship.

### Look at the Task

The workers shared a workplace.

But they did not share the same **task** or **opportunity for exposure**.

**What was different about the work performed by the workers who became ill?**

## Another Observation

Simond noticed something about the rats.

Healthy rats groomed themselves and tended to have relatively few fleas.

Sick rats could no longer groom effectively.

They carried many fleas.

Then the rats died.

### What happened to the fleas?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## A Hypothesis

Simond proposed that an intermediary might connect infected rats with humans.

Perhaps the rat itself was not directly transmitting the disease.

Perhaps the intermediary was a:

### flea

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Testing the Hypothesis

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="55%"}

Simond designed an experiment.

He placed a **plague-infected rat at the bottom of a jar**.

Above it, he suspended a **healthy rat in a wire mesh cage**.

The wire mesh prevented the rats from directly touching.

But fleas could move from the sick rat to the healthy rat.

### The healthy rat developed plague.

## The Control

Simond also tested what happened when fleas were absent.

Without fleas, healthy rats exposed to the experimental conditions did not develop plague.

When fleas were introduced:

### plague appeared.

The transmission pathway was becoming clear.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Transmission Pathway

## Rodent → Flea → Human

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="40%"}

Plague can move between rodents and humans through **fleas**.

A flea feeds on a rodent infected with *Yersinia pestis* and acquires the bacterium.

When an infected rodent becomes sick or dies, fleas may leave the animal and seek another blood meal.

If an infected flea bites a human, *Yersinia pestis* can enter the new host.

### Rodent → Flea → Human

This was the transmission pathway Simond had been investigating.

# Agent, Reservoir, Vector, Susceptible Host

We can now give names to the major parts of an infectious disease transmission system.

## Agent

A microorganism (bacterium, virus, fungus, or other infectious material) that causes disease.

## Reservoir

An organism that maintains a virus or bacterial pathogen in an area by serving as a host.

## Vector

An organism (e.g., insect) that can carry infectious agents from one animal to another.

## Susceptible Host

A person or animal that can become infected by an infectious agent due to lack of immunity.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Understanding the Disease

Now that we know the agent, we can better understand what happens after infection.

*Yersinia pestis* can produce different forms of disease depending on **how the bacteria enter the body and where the infection develops**.

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="40%"}

### Bubonic Plague

Bacteria reach the **lymphatic system** and multiply in regional lymph nodes, producing swollen lymph nodes called **buboes**.

### Septicemic Plague

Bacteria enter the **bloodstream**, producing a severe systemic infection.

### Pneumonic Plague

Bacteria infect the **lungs**.

Respiratory droplets from a person or animal with pneumonic plague can transmit *Yersinia pestis* to another person.

### Same Agent, Different Forms of Disease

The agent remains the same:

***Yersinia pestis***

What changes is the **route of exposure and where infection develops in the body**.

# From Microbiology to Occupational Health

Now consider plague as an occupational health problem.

### Who might encounter this exposure pathway because of their work?

Possible occupational groups include:

- ranchers
- livestock workers
- veterinarians
- veterinary technicians
- animal control workers
- wildlife biologists
- agricultural workers
- pest control workers

The occupational hazard is not determined by the job title alone.

It depends on the **task and opportunity for exposure**.

## Think Like an Exposure Scientist

For any biological hazard, ask:

- What is the agent?
- Where is the reservoir?
- How does it leave the reservoir?
- How does it move through the environment?
- How does it reach the worker?
- What route allows it to enter the body

# Plague in the United States

:::: {.columns}
::: {.column width="40%"}

Plague still occurs naturally in parts of the **western United States**.

*Yersinia pestis* circulates among wild rodents and their fleas.

Humans and domestic animals can be exposed when infected fleas seek another host.

This means plague remains relevant to:

- wildlife work
- veterinary work
- outdoor work
- animal handling
- public health surveillance

Although human plague is uncommon, recognizing the **agent, reservoir, vector, and susceptible host** helps us understand where exposure can occur and how it can be prevented.

# Key Takeaway

The disease existed before we understood it.

The exposure pathway existed before we understood it.

The hazard did not suddenly appear when scientists identified it.

### Our ability to observe, measure, and explain it changed.

Plague provides a historical example of how **observation, technology, experimentation, microbiology, and exposure science** can help us understand a biological hazard.

In occupational health, identifying the hazard is only the beginning.

We also need to understand:

- where it comes from
- how it moves
- who encounters it
- how exposure occurs
- what happens after exposure
- where we can intervene

# Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Germ theory** | The scientific concept that microorganisms can cause disease. |
| **Pathogen** | A biological agent capable of causing disease. |
| **Agent** | A microorganism or other infectious material that causes disease. |

| **Transmission route** | The way an infectious agent moves from its source or reservoir to a susceptible host. |
| **Vector-borne transmission** | Transmission of an infectious agent through a living vector. |
