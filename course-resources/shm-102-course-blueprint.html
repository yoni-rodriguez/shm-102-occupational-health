SHM 102 Course Blueprint

Author

Yoni Rodriguez

Course Purpose

SHM 102 introduces students to the foundational concepts and reasoning used to understand occupational health problems. The course develops a common language for recognizing occupational hazards, understanding how workers are exposed, identifying potential health effects, evaluating factors that influence risk, and considering strategies for prevention and control.

Rather than examining workplace hazards in isolation, students explore how worker health is shaped by interactions among environmental conditions, public policy, communities, workplaces, individual behaviors and susceptibility, and biological processes.

By the end of the course, students should be able to recognize occupational health problems, investigate how and why they occur, evaluate evidence, and propose practical approaches for protecting worker health.

Course Philosophy

Occupational health problems rarely exist in isolation. SHM 102 therefore uses a systems based, case study approach rather than organizing the course exclusively around individual hazard categories.

The course moves repeatedly between scales:

Climate & Environment
       ↓
Society & Policy
       ↓
Community Health
       ↓
Workplace Environment
       ↓
Worker Health & Behaviors
       ↓
Cells, Molecules & Microorganisms

Students practice a common occupational health reasoning framework:

Worker → Task → Hazard → Exposure → Health Effect → Risk → Controls

Core Concepts

Students develop foundations in occupational and environmental health, history and policy, major hazard categories, exposure pathways and routes, dose and exposure characteristics, risk and susceptibility, prevention, and the hierarchy of controls.

Instructional Progression

Foundations

The opening portion establishes the conceptual and historical background students need before independently analyzing occupational health problems. Foundations include what occupational health is, connections to environmental health and public health, historical development, institutions and policy, hazard categories, hazard versus risk, exposure pathways and routes, prevention, hierarchy of controls, and the occupational health reasoning framework.

Case Based Weekly Rhythm

After foundations, a typical week may move from a real problem or case, to scientific concepts, to investigation and application, and finally to synthesis, discussion, or project work. This rhythm is intentionally flexible. The syllabus should identify major milestones and university closures without locking the course into exact weekly topic titles while the first redesigned offering is being developed.

Reading and Discussion Days

The Fall 2026 section may periodically use a class meeting as a seminar style reading discussion. Students may read selected excerpts or short materials connected to occupational health cases and then discuss the worker, task, hazard, exposure, health effect, risk, and control implications.

One planned example is the opening chapter of The Hot Zone, used to discuss occupational exposure, field work, infectious disease, and what exposure science can look like outside the United States. Copyrighted readings will be provided through Canvas or another permitted university access method and will not be redistributed through the public course repository.

Reflections

Short Canvas reflections may follow readings, discussions, cases, or other class activities. These are primarily completion based. A student receives credit for a genuine, responsive attempt that engages with the prompt. Blank, unrelated, or nonresponsive submissions do not receive credit. Reflections contribute to Engagement & Current Events rather than functioning as separate high stakes writing assignments.

Midterm: Foundations

The midterm assesses students’ understanding of foundational language and concepts needed to analyze occupational health problems. Students should recognize and use concepts rather than rely exclusively on memorized definitions. Potential areas include occupational and environmental health, hazard categories, hazard versus risk, exposure pathways and routes, dose, frequency, duration, intensity, health effects, susceptibility, prevention, hierarchy of controls, and occupational health reasoning.

Tentative timing: Week 5. The exact date will be posted in Canvas.

Engagement & Current Events

Engagement activities provide opportunities to recognize, discuss, investigate, and apply occupational health concepts. Activities may include current event analyses, case discussions, assigned readings, Poll Everywhere, small group exercises, research activities, Canvas reflections, final presentation peer review, audience participation, and other in class participation activities.

Engagement emphasizes meaningful participation and application rather than attendance alone.

Final Group Project

The final group project is the primary application based assessment. Students collaboratively investigate a real occupational or environmental health problem and apply the reasoning framework developed throughout the quarter.

Students should be able to define the problem, identify affected workers or populations, describe relevant work and tasks, identify hazards, explain exposure pathways, evaluate health consequences and factors influencing risk, use credible evidence, recommend prevention and control strategies, apply the hierarchy of controls where appropriate, communicate findings clearly, and explain how an intervention could be evaluated.

Project development occurs progressively through the quarter. Tentative milestones include group and topic selection, evidence search, exposure and health effect analysis, controls and recommendations, presentation development, feedback, and revision. Exact milestone dates will be posted in Canvas.

Inclement Weather and Transportation Disruptions

If weather or transportation conditions make an in person meeting impractical or unsafe, the class may move to a remote format or an asynchronous Canvas activity may replace that meeting. Changes will be communicated through Canvas as early as reasonably possible.

Role of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is not prohibited in SHM 102. Students are encouraged to use AI thoughtfully as a tool for learning, brainstorming, clarifying concepts, improving questions, and supporting their understanding.

Students remain responsible for the accuracy, interpretation, reasoning, and recommendations contained in work they submit. AI should support rather than replace the student’s own thinking. When AI materially contributes to submitted work, students should briefly identify the tool and explain how it was used. Students should verify factual claims and sources rather than assuming generated content is accurate.

Use of AI to misrepresent another person’s or a tool’s work as the student’s own, to fabricate evidence or citations, or to bypass the learning purpose of an assignment may constitute academic dishonesty.

Student Onboarding

Lecture 00, How to Succeed in SHM 102, will introduce how the course works, expectations for participation and communication, office hours, grade questions, major assessments, the final project, study strategies, and campus resources. It will draw on practical ideas about planning, focused work, retrieval practice, spacing, and active learning.

Course Repository and Restricted Materials

The public SHM 102 repository may include instructor created lecture QMD files, rendered HTML lectures, figures, student facing assignment instructions, rubrics, vocabulary resources, and other publicly distributable materials.

Student information, student response data, grades, restricted examination questions, answer keys, internal university records, and copyrighted third party materials that cannot legally be redistributed must remain outside the public repository.
