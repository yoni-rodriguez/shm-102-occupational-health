SHM 102 Course Blueprint

Author

Yoni Rodriguez

Course Purpose

SHM 102 introduces students to the foundational concepts and reasoning used to understand occupational health problems.

The course develops a common language for recognizing occupational hazards, understanding how workers are exposed, identifying potential health effects, evaluating factors that influence risk, and considering strategies for prevention and control.

Rather than examining workplace hazards in isolation, students explore how worker health is shaped by interactions among environmental conditions, public policy, communities, workplaces, individual behaviors and susceptibility, and biological processes.

By the end of the course, students should be able to recognize occupational health problems, investigate how and why they occur, evaluate evidence, and propose practical approaches for protecting worker health.

Course Philosophy

Occupational health problems rarely exist in isolation. Worker exposures are shaped by environmental conditions, laws and policies, community conditions, workplace organization, job tasks, individual characteristics, and biological processes.

SHM 102 therefore uses a systems-based, case-study approach rather than organizing the course exclusively around individual hazard categories. Chemical, biological, physical, ergonomic, and psychosocial hazards remain foundational concepts, but students encounter these hazards within real occupational health problems.

The course moves repeatedly between scales:

Climate & Environment ↓ Society & Policy ↓ Community Health ↓ Workplace Environment ↓ Worker Health & Behaviors ↓ Cells, Molecules & Microorganisms

Students will examine cases from global, national, regional, and local contexts and practice asking the same fundamental questions across different problems:

Worker → Task → Hazard → Exposure → Health Effect → Risk → Controls

The goal is not simply for students to memorize lists of hazards or regulations. The goal is to develop the ability to recognize an occupational health problem, ask useful questions, evaluate evidence, understand connections across scales, and reason toward practical approaches for prevention.

How Scale Organizes the Course

The scale framework provides an organizing structure for examining occupational health problems from broad environmental and societal forces to workplace conditions, individual workers, and biological mechanisms.

The scales are interconnected rather than a strictly one-way causal pathway. Occupational health cases may move upward or downward across multiple scales depending on the problem being examined.

Design Rationale

Climate change begins the scale-based portion of the course because it allows students to move from a global environmental driver through societal, community, workplace, worker, and biological consequences.

Beginning at this broad scale establishes the central idea that occupational health does not begin and end at the workplace. Changes in climate can influence land use, food systems, migration, working and living conditions, heat exposure, air quality, vector ecology, infectious disease, and other factors that ultimately shape worker health.

The course can then progressively move across smaller scales while repeatedly showing students how those scales remain connected.

Occupational Health Reasoning Framework

Throughout the course, occupational health cases will repeatedly be examined using a common framework:

Worker → Task → Hazard → Exposure → Health Effect → Risk → Controls

This framework provides students with a consistent approach for identifying and analyzing occupational health problems across different hazards, workplaces, populations, and scales.

Assessment Architecture

SHM 102 uses a 400-point course structure emphasizing foundational knowledge, engagement with occupational health issues, and application of course concepts to a real-world problem.

Midterm — Foundations

Points: 100

The midterm assesses students’ understanding of the foundational language and concepts needed to analyze occupational health problems.

Engagement & Current Events

Points: 100

Engagement activities provide opportunities for students to recognize, discuss, and apply occupational health concepts throughout the quarter.

Activities may include current-event analyses, case discussions, readings, peer review, and other course participation activities.

Final Group Project

Points: 200

The final group project serves as the primary application-based assessment for the course. Students work collaboratively to investigate a real occupational or environmental health problem and apply the concepts and reasoning framework developed throughout the quarter.

Final Project Structure

Group Organization

  • Approximately 60 students
  • Approximately 5 students per group
  • Approximately 12 project groups

Case Framework

Final-project cases will use the same occupational health reasoning framework practiced throughout the course:

What's happening?
       ↓
Who is affected?
       ↓
What is the hazard?
       ↓
How are people exposed?
       ↓
What are the health consequences?
       ↓
What factors make the problem better or worse?
       ↓
What controls would you recommend?
       ↓
How would you know whether the intervention worked?

Library Research Sessions

Two library research sessions will introduce students to strategies for finding and evaluating evidence for their final group projects.

Approximately half of the class will attend each session.

The library sessions should occur early in the quarter, after students have been assigned their groups and project topics, so students can immediately apply research skills to their own occupational health problem.

Student Onboarding

Welcome to SHM 102

A short student-facing course orientation will introduce students to the structure of SHM 102, expectations for participation, major assessments, course resources, and the approach used to examine occupational health problems.

Who Is SHM 102? Survey

An introductory survey will be used to better understand the backgrounds and interests represented in the class.

Potential information may include:

  • Class standing
  • Major or academic program
  • Previous work experience
  • Previous exposure to occupational or environmental health
  • Occupational health topics of interest
  • Students’ initial understanding of occupational health
  • What students hope to learn during the course

Aggregate results may be incorporated into an early course meeting to introduce the class to the range of backgrounds, experiences, and interests represented in SHM 102.
